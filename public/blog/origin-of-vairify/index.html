The Origin of VAIRIFY

How a tragedy in 2020 led to a question about anonymity and accountability—and ultimately to V.A.I., ChainPass, and VAIRIFY.

By Brian Anthony · Founder, VAIRIFYPart one of four

The Catalytic Event

In the spring of 2020, with much of the world shut down, a story about a young woman in Chicago named Elizabeth Long came through my Facebook feed.

Elizabeth was a 22-year-old mother and a sex worker. She had been killed in a hotel room. The man accused of killing her allegedly took her phone and used it to set up and rob several more clients.

The comments were cold and dismissive. A few even made jokes. But I could not stop thinking about one thing: why would anyone feel comfortable using their victim’s phone after a murder to commit more crimes in the same community?

Understanding the Real Problem

That question sent me into research.

The threats were different, but they kept pointing to the same thing: no real accountability. The price could be as high as the one Elizabeth Long paid. But even a misdemeanor could cast a shadow over a person’s life or destroy it completely. People had good reason to protect their identities.

Anonymity was not the problem. The absence of accountability was.

How could a person be anonymous and accountable at the same time?

Finding the Answer

I called Jake, a friend with a background in blockchain technology, and asked him to help me solve it. We researched everything—blockchain, encryption, and technologies far beyond anything commonly available at the time. None of them could provide accountability while keeping legal identity information away from the platform. I wanted a structure in which VAIRIFY could not disclose information it never received and a member’s legal identity would remain theirs to reveal voluntarily.

Then I opened an account with Binance. I filled out my information and was sent to a KYC provider, where I submitted my driver’s license and took a live photo. The entire process took about four minutes.

In 2020, KYC was used mostly by financial institutions and governments to validate important transactions and onboard new customers. Today, it is commonplace and considered one of the most accurate and secure identity-verification processes available.

And I thought: what if there were no reason to submit any personal information to us at all? Validating the information on a government-issued ID should be enough to prove the person is real, and the live selfie should prove that the person presenting the ID is the person pictured on it.

We did not need the name, address, document number, or any of the other personal information. We only needed confirmation that the person was real and the verified photo produced by the verification process.

Later that night, I picked up my phone and used Face ID. It recognized me instantly.

It clicked.

If we combined the two facial-recognition technologies, we could use the same secure process banks trust to prove a person is real, then use the technology millions of us use every day to unlock our phones to confirm that person again in seconds by comparing a new live image with the verified photo produced during the verification process.

I called Jake. He suggested creating a unique seven-character alphanumeric identifier and cryptographically attaching it to the photo. We could attach a history—transactions, contracts, or any other record—to that number, giving us the accountability we needed, then verify the person behind it with a facial scan without ever receiving their personal information.

The combination of the verified photo and number would become a Verified Anonymous Identity—a V.A.I.

The Research

I started looking at what was already available.

There was one company that specialized in verification for the sex work community, so I joined and went through the process. Membership was $190 for the year. I submitted all of my personal information and waited about 24 hours. When the process was complete, I received a number. Four of the digits in that number came directly from my Social Security number.

Eventually, I realized the number was just my ticket into their community.

Over the next 18 months, I interviewed 23 providers, 7 clients, and 4 law-enforcement officers. It was COVID. Much of the world was shut down, and I had the time to listen.

Only four providers and one client belonged to the verified community. I am sure that was because I had set the interviews up through the community. One of the providers I interviewed was kind enough to give my information to one of her clients, who contacted me.

Almost everyone else was using a different form of verification on their own. Some used social media, some asked a client to repeat a specific phrase, and some used government-issued ID. Everyone had a different method and a different threshold for what they considered enough.

A few things became evident. Verification was a problem, but it was just the tip of the iceberg. Every method took time, every method had holes, and the process had to happen with every new encounter.

The list of problems and complaints grew, but eventually a clear pattern formed. They fell into two categories: safety and convenience.

Everyone had a story. A close call. A friend who had not been as lucky. Or a complaint. A request that went unanswered for hours. A calendar that needed a workaround.

By the end of those interviews, I was looking at a community whose problems had largely gone unaddressed.

The Revelation

Several things happened during those 18 months that began pointing in the same direction.

One was a conversation with a lifelong friend who worked in law enforcement. He told me that, for the most part, law enforcement was not focused on sex work itself. It was focused on the crime surrounding it.

Then, while visiting my 80-year-old mother in Las Vegas, she took me to a marijuana dispensary.

The feeling was surreal. I found myself standing with my 80-year-old mother in one of the largest marijuana dispensaries in the world. I would bet my life that my mother had never touched marijuana. Just five years earlier, she would never have stepped foot in a place like it.

But when I looked around, I saw ordinary people—doctors, schoolteachers, secretaries. People who, only a few years earlier, might never have openly associated themselves with marijuana.

The marijuana had not changed. The framework around it had.

That framework had changed who felt comfortable participating, how the activity was perceived, and where the criminal element could operate.

That was the revelation.

For the first time, it became clear that verification alone would solve some of the problems that had plagued the sex work community for decades. But it would not solve the larger problem.

The community needed a complete framework.

V.A.I. could provide the foundation: verified identity, age, continuity, and accountability while preserving anonymity. VAIRIFY could build the framework around it.

It would not end scams, underage participation, violence, or the other criminal activity surrounding sex work. Those things would still exist, but they would exist outside VAIRIFY.

The scam that happened to me made that easy to understand. It worked because the scammers had my real phone number. From that, they found my address and my family and turned that information into a threat. Inside VAIRIFY, they would have had my V.A.I.—an anonymous credential with nothing they could use to identify or threaten me. And before they could ever reach me, they would have had to verify themselves.

Underage participation was just as straightforward. Age would be established before a V.A.I. was issued. A minor could not enter the VAIRIFY Framework as a verified adult.

No framework can eliminate every crime or every act of violence. But the VAIRIFY Framework changes the equation for premeditated bad actors: to operate inside it, they have to identify themselves, become accountable, and permanently attach their activity to a V.A.I., while the member they approach remains anonymous. For most premeditated crime, that destroys the advantage before the crime ever begins.

The Decision to Build

By the time the interviews were complete, Jake and I had decided to build more than a verification system. We would build an application that addressed every problem on our list.

I am not a coder, and the development tools available today did not exist yet. The estimate came back in the mid-six figures, so we brought in a third partner to help carry the financial load.

Six months later, the application was nearing completion and we had begun preparing for launch.

The Split

Specialized legal counsel raised a problem we had not solved. Even if VAIRIFY did not intend to expose a member’s identity, the identity technology and the community platform should not remain under the same structure.

The lawyers suggested separating the identity technology from VAIRIFY. Jake and I agreed.

The split was natural. ChainPass became the technology company behind V.A.I., while VAIRIFY remained focused on the sex work community and the problems I had set out to solve.

Jake immediately saw V.A.I. as technology that could work across many unrelated platforms. That broader use also strengthened the anonymity model: possessing a V.A.I. would not, by itself, identify a member as part of VAIRIFY.

The ChainPass Structure

When I stepped away from VAIRIFY, Jake continued developing ChainPass and expanding what V.A.I. could become.

The separation had already given the two companies different jobs. VAIRIFY remained focused on this community. ChainPass would take V.A.I. to other platforms.

We signed a licensing agreement that allowed ChainPass to use and expand the V.A.I. technology, and I saw an opportunity to build something else into that relationship: a way for VAIRIFY to continue funding free safety as ChainPass grew.

Under the agreement, ChainPass pays 10 percent of each completed V.A.I. to the individual who referred the user.

Separately, ChainPass pays 20 percent of its V.A.I. revenue to VAIRIFY, including revenue from V.A.I.s created through other participating platforms. That 20 percent was designed to help cover VAIRIFY’s operating costs as ChainPass expanded, protecting our ability to keep VAIRIFY’s safety features free.

That was intentional.

If V.A.I. eventually worked across dozens or hundreds of unrelated platforms, VAIRIFY could participate in that growth without having to turn safety into the product it sold to its own members.

The larger ChainPass became, the stronger the financial foundation behind VAIRIFY’s promise of free safety could become.

That structure also let us make several commitments designed to remove the barriers I had heard about throughout the interviews. Platforms would not be charged simply to incorporate V.A.I. A successful referral would generate a 10 percent payment to the individual who made it. The V.A.I. price for VAIRIFY users would not increase. A user coming to V.A.I. through VAIRIFY could defer payment for 48 hours. And once a member had a V.A.I., that same credential could be used across participating platforms rather than requiring another verification every time.

There was still one issue we could not agree on.

I had decided that VAIRIFY’s safety features would be free. Our financial partner believed those features should be part of the revenue model.

Neither of us was willing to move.

For me, free safety had become part of the foundation of VAIRIFY. Changing it would mean changing the reason I had started building the platform in the first place.

So I returned his investment.

Financially, that decision hurt. But I would rather have absorbed the loss than build a platform that put a price between a member and the protections we had spent years creating.

I had no idea that within days, money would become the least important thing on my mind.

The Pause

Less than four days later, during what was supposed to be a routine doctor visit, my wife was diagnosed with ALS—a terminal neuromuscular disease with no known cure.

Everything changed.

VAIRIFY was close to completion. The application was working. Outreach had begun. We had produced the launch material. We were preparing to introduce something we had spent years researching and building.

And then none of it seemed important.

I stopped.

Not because the project had failed. Quite the opposite. We were close.

I stopped because there are moments when life makes the decision for you.

The evidence of how close we came is still there.

The original VAIRIFY Reddit account is still online, almost untouched since we stepped away.

Until I was ready to finish it.
